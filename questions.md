# Questions

Fifty scenarios. Each one is sent to the Skillify API with Qwen as the backend, before the edit and again after it. Astra does not see this file. Each question is one paragraph. The answer is the market narrative and the trades.

1. GDP growth has accelerated for two quarters, and inflation has accelerated with it. The dollar is still inside one annualized-vol band of its level a year ago. One-month equity momentum is positive, but implied vol has left the bottom third of its five-year range. What is the current market narrative, and what trades would you put in the book?

2. The dollar is late in a multi-year up cycle. The euro, the yen, and a basket of Asian currencies all sit cheap on real exchange rates, by roughly similar amounts. Your conviction is real, but not high enough to flip the whole book onto one side. What is the narrative through this transition, and how do you split the book between the dollar and the assets against it?

3. You are long a six-month dollar view, and that view has not changed. Realized vol on the book has doubled from one week to the next, twice in a month. At full size, a two-week flush would hit the stop even if the six-month view is right. What is the narrative, and how do you size and structure the book through this stretch?

4. Carry-to-vol on a high-yielding Asian currency against the dollar is well above one. That currency sells off hard in risk-off, and its central bank has started to lean against the spot move. What is the narrative, and do you put the carry trade on?

5. You are receiving the US two-year yield. Two-week realized vol on that yield is the lowest in a year, about a third of the vol you would use for a six-month hold. What narrative explains the calm, and how much leverage belongs on the trade?

6. A liquid emerging-market currency is three standard deviations from the level its policy rate and current account would suggest. You can see the gap on the screen. You do not yet have a story for the flow that caused it. What is the narrative, and what do you trade?

7. Your case is that the dollar stays firm because US real rates stay above Europe and Japan. That view is already high conviction. A vote of one-month moves across the liquid dollar pairs is still split, and the momentum screens have not lined up. What narrative are you trading, and do you get ahead of the breadth or wait?

8. The dollar index is up about 2% over three sessions, and one-week dollar vol has jumped with it. Yields have barely moved, and you cannot name a policy or growth surprise that should still be in force next month. What is the narrative around this break, and what, if anything, do you chase?

9. You are short gamma. In won, the Taiwan dollar, and rupiah, selling the at-the-money straddle has been the better sale, and you can stay long the skew. In euro and yen, selling the wings has paid more than selling at-the-money. What is the narrative in each surface, and how do you build the short-gamma book?

10. At-the-money vol across the G10 is trading as one contagion factor. Sterling's smile is rich against its own at-the-money, and that richness has lined up poorly with the rest of the short-vol book. What is the narrative, and do you add the smile as a second book?

11. Euro front-end vol inverted during last week's spike and has started to come off. One-year euro vol is still sticky. Holding that long vega as it rolls down a steep curve is expensive. What is the narrative for the curve, and where do you sell vega?

12. Euro one-month implied vol is near 6 and the yen's is near 12. You want a relative-value vol trade between them. Equal notional would leave you much longer yen vega than euro vega. What is the narrative, and how do you weight the two legs?

13. Most of the risk is unhedged one-month at-the-money straddles in euro, sterling, and yen, and you are short them. The euro risk reversal has moved about two vols toward euro puts, and nothing hedges it. What is the narrative, and how do you rebuild the front-end short?

14. Equities are down, credit spreads are wider, and dollar-yen has flipped from risk-on to risk-off over a week. You had been selling the expensive dollar-yen topside against at-the-money. The weak yen wing is now the cheap one. What is the narrative, and which wing do you sell?

15. A tariff headline has spiked one-week euro gamma, and the broker will not make a market for half an hour. You still own one-month euro topside as the disaster hedge, and the euro vol curve is steep. What is the narrative, and what do you fade?

16. At-the-money vol is unchanged, but the 10-delta wing has exploded. The overlay that sells at-the-money and buys the topside now costs more than it used to. What is the narrative, and do you keep the overlay, take the profit, or run the short without it?

17. Hog futures are two standard deviations off the seasonal path. Weather in the main supply region has been poor, and finished weights usually respond four to six months later, not in the next print. What is the narrative, and do you open a fundamental trade or keep seasonality as the base?

18. A proven retailer is down hard after a botched acquisition. The multiple is below its usual range. You can name two operating levers that would close the gap to similar stores. The sellers are funds that have to cut. What is the narrative, and is this a buy?

19. An analyst wants a large overweight in a copper-demand proxy for a set of miners. The series is a private survey you cannot back-test, and the end-market it tracks is a small share of those companies' profit. What is the narrative around that factor, and what weight does it get in the book?

20. You are long a US industrial on a two-quarter earnings case. A block print just went through the other way, about two days' volume. It may be profit-taking, a pair against a peer, a hedge, or a fund on a one-week clock, not a changed view of the business. What is the narrative for that flow, and does it change the trade?

21. A listed food-delivery company looks like the winner on users and margin. The competitor that sets price in its biggest city is private, and you cannot see that company's orders or cash. What is the narrative, and how large a winner-take-all position is allowed?

22. A private software company is being priced at a high multiple of sales. The buyers are funds with a long public record in that sector. You cannot point to who at the table is wrong. What is the narrative, and what do you do with a position in the listed peers?

23. You cover a global consumer company on a three-month horizon. The market keeps score with the US retail slice, which is not most of earnings. A ten-year sum of the parts would weight the US much less and the emerging-market stores much more. What is the narrative, and which slice do you weight in the trade?

24. The research case on a payments company is a ten-year compounder, with most of the value in the later years. The book that would hold it is judged every month and is allowed to be long or short. What is the narrative, and which strategy and timeframe get this name?

25. A value retailer has raised list prices. The next two quarters of earnings look better. The brand's place was cheap and fast, and the gap to peers has narrowed. What is the narrative, and how do you trade the near-term earnings against the longer position?

26. Cloud software is leaving the early-adopter years. A few years ago, revenue growth in the sector lifted almost every listed name. Customers are now choosing one vendor, and the earnings are concentrating. What is the narrative now, and which companies, if any, do you still own?

27. A listed software company earns operating margins near 40%, against competitors near 15%, and revenue growth is still above 20%. Those margins are visible to anyone who might enter. What is the narrative, and how does that change the size and the direction of the position?

28. Two stocks in one cyclical sector are each down by half. In the first, the balance sheet is stretched, but the plants and the customers are intact. In the second, the product no longer sells. What is the narrative for each, and which one do you buy?

29. A software issuer's convertible screens ten vols cheap. The company is light on assets and still growing fast, and you would not want to own the credit if the option ends worthless. What is the narrative, and do you buy the cheap option?

30. A regulator has said the shares can be delisted if price-to-book stays below one. The large-cap stocks are the obvious buy. Some convertibles, mostly quieter smaller names, still sit at a deep discount to that floor. What is the narrative, and which market do you buy?

31. A government is refinancing a growing pile of debt from cheap old coupons into much higher market rates. It still runs a deficit, and companies have started issuing equity again. You cannot name the buyer who has to take the new paper. What is the narrative, and what do you own against that?

32. You have underwritten a high-premium, low-delta convertible. There is no natural buyer until the premium falls back into a long-only index, or until the credit can be hedged. What is the narrative, and is this a carry trade or a quick re-rating?

33. A convertible from a mid-size industrial has traded cheap because hedge funds could not borrow the stock. The bond was several points under where a hedged buyer would pay. Borrow has just opened. What is the narrative, and what do you do with the cheapness?

34. Long-only convertible funds have been redeemed. What is left is hedge funds and pod shops. The old habit was to fade a bond above 140. Their screens want the high-delta bond with a cheap embedded put. What is the narrative, and do you still fade that print?

35. Five-year implied vol has broken out of its long range, and realized vol has broken out with it. New convertible issues are being priced richer because buyers cannot get enough vega. What is the narrative, and how large a long-vega book do you run?

36. Pod shops are the bid in high-delta convertibles. Their stops sit close together, so a down day could force a waterfall. Five-year vega on those bonds is still rich to realized. What is the narrative, and do you buy that vega now or wait until it is cheap?

37. For the same credit quality, Chinese convertible bonds pay more spread than bonds in the United States or Europe. Global mandates are not yet forced to own that region. What is the narrative, and do you keep the overweight for the extra carry?

38. Gold is down about 12% from the recent high. Central banks and other official buyers are still adding to reserves. The US ten-year yield is testing the high it made in the last panic. What is the narrative, and do you put the gold risk back on?

39. A convertible yields more than the same issuer's straight bond. The pickup is small. You cannot finance the pair, and a cash credit desk would not get approval to do it for that much extra yield. What is the narrative, and is the pickup a trade?

40. The cash price and the hedge of a stock are far enough apart that relative-value accounts will trade the basis. Lending data says that trade creates a bid for local-currency funding. The same day, the central bank is adding reserves and the banks are flushing seasonal cash. What is the narrative, and do you trade the funding?

41. Dollar-won forwards, one month and shorter, are moving with a large move in US front-end rates. Korean onshore rates have barely changed. What is the narrative for local funding, and do you treat the onshore rate as a clean trade?

42. A copper mine outage has taken a few percent of supply offline. A lot of length is betting the gap lasts several quarters. The first weeks of lost tonnes are real. In past outages, spare capacity and scrap came back faster than that kind of consensus. What is the narrative, and where do you fade it?

43. The consensus is long the dollar for another leg higher. You can see the average entry of the holders, and spot is only a small move away from it. That entry is their pain point. What is the narrative, overreaction or underreaction, and what do you trade?

44. A strike has closed a major oil shipping lane. Oil and the currency of the producer are both a multi-sigma move on the day. You are not close to the people who will decide how long it lasts. Moves of this shape have often faded inside a week. What is the narrative, and do you fade it?

45. Your core view is long US cyclicals against defensives for the next two quarters. That spread has already moved sharply the other way, and it is still going. What is the narrative, and do you fade the extension or leave it alone?

46. The Mexican peso is several standard deviations from its six-month range after a local rates surprise. The argument about the central bank is now beside the point. You can write down what a fade would lose, what it can realistically make, and whether you would take that bet many times a year. What is the narrative, and what size do you trade?

47. You are long a high-yield credit index into a date six months out, and you think the spread ends tighter. The position is leveraged. A widening well short of that destination would wipe the equity in the trade. What is the narrative, and how do you size and stagger the entries so one day is not fatal?

48. You are starting a book of single-name equity trades. An honest hit rate is about half. The book only pays if the winners are several times the size of the losers. What is the narrative for the book, and how do you size each bet?

49. A regional-bank story is spreading through the headlines, and the language gets more extreme each day. The equity index is still making new highs, and bank stocks are not yet the factor driving the tape. What is the narrative, and do you put the crisis trades on now?

50. The tape has started to trade that bank stress. An old hedge that paid in the last funding squeeze, a receiver of short-dated funding stress, is still priced as if the stress does not matter. What is the narrative, and how much of that hedge do you buy?
